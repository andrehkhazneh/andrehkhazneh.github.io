Silence

On the Words We Choose Not to Speak

By Andreh Khazneh · 16 November 2026

Many believe that silence is an inability to express oneself, an emptiness where words should be. What they fail to understand is that silence does not always come from having nothing to say. Sometimes, it comes from carrying more than words could ever hold.

At the very moment when our longing to speak reaches its peak, we may choose silence as our last refuge of composure. It can be a form of strength, something we cling to in order not to fall apart. Yet it can also reveal our helplessness before the sheer magnitude of pain. Perhaps silence is both strength and vulnerability at once, a contradiction whose meaning changes with the reasons behind it.

There are many reasons for silence, and some are deeply painful.

Sometimes, we are afraid to let the words escape because speaking about what happened would make the experience feel even more real, and therefore more unbearable. As long as the pain remains unspoken, it stays hidden from the ears of others. But once we give it a voice, once we see the weight of our suffering reflected in another person's understanding, it can feel as though we are living through it all over again.

In those moments, the gentleness of silence may seem far more merciful than the cruelty of speaking.

At other times, our suffering feels too vast for language to contain. Whatever we say would capture only a fraction of what we have endured. We may struggle to explain the details, not because we have forgotten them, but because even the longest explanation would fall painfully short of the magnitude of the experience itself.

And when words cannot convey the truth as we have lived it, silence, paradoxically, becomes its most faithful translation.

Sometimes, we retreat into silence to protect ourselves from those who might listen.

Imagine opening a window into your soul, trusting someone to receive your pain with understanding, only to discover that they diminish your suffering or compare it to some minor hardship of their own, treating their experience as the measure of every tragedy.

Imagine telling someone, "I was permanently paralyzed in a devastating car accident."

And hearing them reply, "I'm sorry to hear that, but what happened to you is nothing compared to what I went through. I once cut my finger and lost a drop of blood!"

In such a moment, speaking no longer brings relief. Instead, it becomes another burden added to the one you already carry. Rather than finding someone willing to acknowledge the weight of your suffering, you encounter someone who strips it of its significance.

And so, silence may become the last remaining shelter in which you can preserve the dignity of your pain.

Yet silence is not always about protecting ourselves. Sometimes, we remain silent to protect the people we love.

There are those who care for us so deeply that knowing the full extent of our suffering might break their hearts and expose them to anguish of their own. Out of love, and out of fear for their well-being, we choose to conceal our wounds. We wrap ourselves in silence because we cannot bear the thought of seeing our pain reflected in their eyes.

But this raises a troubling question.

If remaining silent with our parents or friends can be an act of love and protection, what happens when the person we are protecting is our life partner?

Is withholding our pain out of concern for her a genuine expression of love? Or might she later see that silence as a form of exclusion, a betrayal of the promise to stand together through joy and sorrow?

Sharing pain is part of the commitment we make to one another. Yet the desire to spare someone we love may lead us to hide the very suffering they would wish to share.

Here, the boundary between protection and isolation becomes far more difficult to define.

Sometimes, pain is not a single event that can be explained in a few sentences. It is an intricate chain of experiences stretching across years, each one connected to those that came before it.

For someone to truly understand what we have endured, we might have to recount an entire history, something resembling a television series unfolding over many seasons.

And when genuine listening is absent, or understanding seems beyond reach, silence can feel like the only choice left.

Still, silence is not a permanent solution, even when circumstances make it seem unavoidable.

The words we bury within ourselves do not disappear simply because we refuse to speak them. They remain there, waiting for the mercy of forgetfulness, hoping that time might soften or erase some part of what we have suffered.

But what happens to us while we wait?

Keeping everything inside is painful. Over time, the burden of concealment may grow until it nearly equals the suffering that caused it in the first place.

Silence requires strength. Strength requires energy. And that energy must come from an exhausted body and a wounded soul.

There is something profoundly unfair about this equation: a person already drained by pain must somehow summon even more strength simply to endure the silence surrounding it.

It is true that time may soften the intensity of suffering, or gradually teach us how to accept it and live alongside it. But time does not necessarily erase what pain has left behind.

The scars of suffering are like marks carved into stone. Their edges may soften with the passing years, but they remain witnesses to what once was.

Some experiences change us so deeply that returning to the person we were before them becomes impossible.

And so, silence may become a painful answer to an even greater pain. It may become another burden to carry, or a temporary refuge where we wait until we find the strength to speak.

Some may call it weakness. Others may see it as strength. Yet its meaning cannot be separated from the experience that gave rise to it, or from the circumstances that made it necessary.

Not every silence carries the same meaning, just as not every person possesses the same capacity to speak, to endure, or to reveal what lies within.

Between what we cannot say, what we fear to say, and what we choose not to say, silence takes on countless forms, leaving us with questions that may never have a single answer.

And perhaps, in certain moments of life, silence is neither a victory over pain nor a surrender to it.

Perhaps it is simply a refuge for those who have survived, a place where they shelter what remains of their souls, so they may find the strength to go on living.
