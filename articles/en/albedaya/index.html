The Beginning: The Missing First Chapter of Existence

How does not knowing the beginning limit our understanding, and why must we return to the roots of a story to judge others fairly?

By Andreh Khazneh · Translated from the Arabic original

Everything has a beginning—or so we have come to believe. The beginning is the first thread we reach for when trying to understand what follows. It may hold the key to the events and outcomes we see today. Yet there is a paradox: for all their importance, beginnings are often the most elusive parts of any story.

The earliest origins of so much remain unknown to us. Looking back toward the dawn of history, we face questions for which we have no definitive answers. And the question may go deeper still: what came before the beginning itself?

Our reasoning, then, is bound by the limits of our knowledge. We try to explain what we witness without knowing everything that came before it.

Imagine opening a novel halfway through.

You would meet its characters and follow its events. You might sympathize with some and condemn others. But soon you would ask: What happened in the opening chapters? How did these characters become who they are now? And what brought the story to this turning point?

Without those answers, you would begin filling the gaps with assumptions of your own. Some may seem so convincing that you forget they are only attempts to explain what you do not know.

More troubling still is what happens when, over time, these interpretations become unquestioned truths.

Someone might adopt an imagined account of an unknown beginning, then pass it on to the next generation as established fact. Their children may accept it with confidence, not necessarily because they have tested its accuracy, but because they inherited it from people they trust. And so an assumption becomes a conviction, and a conviction becomes an unquestioned certainty, until the essential question disappears: How do we know that this is how it began?

In a sense, we wander through labyrinths whose beginnings and exits we cannot always locate: the origins of the universe, the dawn of recorded history, and the first spark behind events that changed human lives.

Between Beginning and End

Amid such vast uncertainty, I find comfort in knowing how at least some things in my own life began. I remember how they came about and the circumstances that shaped them. Sometimes, I can even follow their course to the end.

But knowing the beginning does not necessarily mean being able to predict the end.

Many paths we choose consciously lead us to destinations we never imagined. A single small decision may set off a chain of possibilities, each branching into a different path, with consequences that could change the course of our lives.

Another paradox emerges: we are limited not only by what we do not know of the past, but also by what we cannot know of the future.

We stand between a past we cannot change and a future we cannot predict, trying, in the present, to understand our choices and their consequences.

The Trap of “What If?” and the Allure of Parallel Universes

What if you reached an ending that felt unfair?

Your mind might return to a particular moment: a decision you made, words spoken in haste, or an encounter you wish you had handled differently.

You replay that moment, again and again, until the questions begin:

What if I had not made that decision? What if I had remained silent? What if I had chosen the other path?

You imagine that changing a single detail in the past might have altered everything that followed, leaving you with a present entirely different from the one you know.

But time gives us no way to go back and test those possibilities.

And so the question “What if?” becomes one of the most painful questions we can ask, because it opens doors we cannot walk through.

Perhaps this is why the idea of parallel universes captivates the human imagination. Regardless of the scientific hypotheses surrounding it, we can imagine it as a metaphor for the lives we might have lived.

We might imagine that another version of ourselves, in a parallel world, took the path we abandoned, avoided the mistake we regret, or reached the ending denied to us.

The idea offers the imagination an escape from the harshness of reality, a glimpse of the fairer ending we wish we had reached—even though we have no evidence that such a world exists.

But what if our real need is not to change the ending, but to understand the beginning that led us there?

If we revisit our past and retrace our decisions to understand how we arrived where we are, why are we sometimes unwilling to extend the same understanding to others?

Why do we ask others to consider the circumstances behind our actions, yet sometimes judge someone else by a single moment in their life?

Here, the importance of beginnings moves beyond philosophical reflection and becomes a question of fairness.

Fairness Requires Knowledge

Fairness has always been a human aspiration, but it takes more than good intentions. It calls for knowledge: for understanding how events unfolded before judging their outcome.

How many people have been condemned over incidents whose origins remained unknown? How many stories have taken on a different meaning when missing details finally came to light?

We often rush to judgment based on limited information, incomplete accounts, or potentially misleading claims. Worse, we may form opinions from other people's accounts without asking what those accounts omit.

Imagine a television interview between a host and a guest.

Midway through the conversation, the guest makes a remark. Someone cuts that remark out of context and shares a short clip without showing what was said before or after it.

People watch it. Some grow angry and rush to condemn the guest, perhaps even making serious accusations.

But what if they watched the entire conversation from the beginning?

They might discover that the remark was an answer to a different question, that its meaning changed when taken out of context, or that the missing conversation was essential to understanding the speaker's intent.

Their judgment might then change completely.

This simple example points to a larger problem in how we understand events. We see an outcome without knowing its causes. We hear a statement without its context. We witness an ending, then judge those involved without knowing how the story began.

Understanding how something began does not necessarily justify what followed or absolve anyone of responsibility for their actions. It does, however, allow us to reach a fuller understanding and make less hasty judgments.

Fairness begins with recognizing that what we know may not be all we need to know—not with rushing to a verdict.

Returning to the Starting Point

And so, however far our questions take us, we find ourselves returning to the same idea: the beginning.

We search for it when trying to understand the universe, revisit it when reflecting on our choices, and need it when seeking to treat others fairly.

We may never recover every missing chapter, uncover every hidden cause, or change the endings we have reached. But we can at least be humble before what we do not know and refuse to turn the limits of our knowledge into absolute certainty.

If we hope to be understood when our actions are misread, fairness demands that we offer others the same consideration and listen to their stories before reducing them to a single moment.

The point is not to justify every action or dismiss responsibility. It is to distinguish understanding a person's reasons from excusing their actions—and seeking the truth from rushing to judgment.

We may never know how everything began, but we can resist treating our ignorance as certainty.

Before condemning someone for an action or judging them by a single word, we should remember that we may have read only one page of a much longer story.

For fairness does not begin with judging the ending, but with trying to understand the beginning.
